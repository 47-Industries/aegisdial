# Romance scam — what to do if you've already sent money

A romance scam is the hardest kind to name, because what hurts most isn't the money — it's that it felt real. You're not the first person this happened to, and you won't be the last. The next step is not shame. It's a clean, quiet plan to protect what's left.

## What happens in this scam

You connected through a dating app, Facebook, Instagram, or a "wrong number" text. Over weeks or months, a relationship built through constant messaging — but somehow they were always too busy or traveling to video call. Then a crisis: a medical bill, a stuck shipment, a frozen bank account, a customs fee on a gift for you. The asks compound. Often it escalates into a "crypto opportunity" once the trust is total.

## Why it worked on you (it's not your fault)

Real attachment forms even in fake relationships. Once you've defended this person to your family and invested emotionally, admitting it's a scam feels like losing them twice. The people who run this are organized crews with scripts, photo packs, and handlers. It is not your intelligence on trial. It is their business model.

## What to do in the next 24 hours

1. Stop sending money today. Do not respond to any new "urgent" story — paying it never ends the cycle.
2. Do a reverse-image search on their photos on Google Lens or TinEye. Most scammers reuse stolen images. Keep the screenshots.
3. If you wired money recently, call your bank on the number on the back of your card. Recalls are possible only in a narrow window.
4. If you sent crypto, save the transaction IDs and wallet addresses. File at ic3.gov — crypto traces can sometimes support recovery or charging.
5. File at reportfraud.ftc.gov. Add your state AG if the amount was significant.
6. Change passwords on email, bank, and social accounts. Pull 2FA off SMS onto an authenticator app.
7. Tell one trusted friend or family member. Not to be shamed — to break the isolation the scammer engineered. You will be targeted again if you stay isolated.

## Red flags you might have missed (for next time)

- Never able to video call clearly or meet in person despite "falling in love."
- Claimed to be on an oil rig, in the military overseas, a doctor working abroad, or a widowed engineer.
- First money request was small and "urgent" — a test.
- Asked you to keep the relationship a secret from family.
- Money had to go by wire, gift card, or crypto, never a regular transfer.
