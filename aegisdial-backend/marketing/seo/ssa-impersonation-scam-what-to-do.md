# Social Security scam call — what to do now

If a Social Security scam call rattled you into sharing your SSN or moving money, you need a plan for the next 15 minutes, not tomorrow. The first tell — "your SSN has been suspended" — is something the SSA literally cannot do. But recovery starts now.

## What happens in this scam

A caller claims your Social Security number has been "suspended" or linked to crimes, often drug trafficking or money found in a rental car in Texas. They transfer you to a fake "officer" who says you must verify your SSN and move funds into a government-controlled account to protect them. From there, you get walked to a bank or a Bitcoin ATM and the money is extracted under the "protection" frame.

## Why it worked on you (it's not your fault)

Most Americans don't know the SSA cannot suspend a Social Security number — that phrase alone is the tell, but most people have never had reason to learn it. The scam uses authority, fear, and the specificity of a scripted "Texas rental car" story to sound official in seconds. It works on retired professionals, teachers, and accountants. Daily.

## What to do in the next 15 minutes

1. Hang up. Don't call any number the caller gave you.
2. If money moved, call your bank immediately on the number from the back of your card and ask about a wire or Zelle recall. Windows are hours.
3. If you shared your SSN, go to identitytheft.gov and start the IRS / SSA identity theft recovery workflow. It's free.
4. Place a fraud alert and a credit freeze at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Freezes are free.
5. Open a my Social Security account at the official SSA website to see if anyone has already touched your record, and lock down direct deposit.
6. File at reportfraud.ftc.gov. The SSA OIG also takes reports at its official hotline.
7. Tell one family member. Scam lists recirculate — stop the second call before it arrives.

## Red flags you might have missed (for next time)

- Said your SSN was "suspended" — this is not a real thing; SSNs are not suspended.
- Claimed your SSN was linked to crimes or a rental car in Texas (a scripted detail).
- Told you to move money into a "protected" government account.
- Used a robocall opener with a press-one-to-speak-to-an-officer menu.
- Caller ID spoofed a government-sounding number.
